# The Selection Journey: AIO to IOI — Speaker Notes

**Slide 1 · The Selection Journey**

This session introduces how Australia picks its four-student IOI team. The goal: every student knows which stage comes next for them, when it happens, and what they need to learn for it. Dates and numbers are for the 2026 cycle and change a little each year.

**Slide 2 · Four stages to the IOI team**

Walk left to right. Only the AIO is open: anyone can enter through their school. Every later stage is invitation only. The EGOI is the European Girls' Olympiad in Informatics; its four-person team is picked from AIIO results. The four colours used here stay fixed for the rest of the deck: teal = AIO, orange = School of Excellence, coral = AIIO and FARIO, navy = Selection School.

**Slide 3 · The year at a glance**

One full cycle runs from one August to the next. The highlighted row is the hard gate, covered on the next slide. Sources: AMT pages for the AIO and the Selection School; the AIOC contest schedule for the AIIO and FARIO dates.

**Slide 4 · Year 11 is the cut-off**

This is the single most important fact for planning. A student who first does well in the AIO in Year 12 is too late for this cycle. A Year 11 student who reaches the December school can still sit the AIIO and attend the Selection School in Year 12.

**Slide 5 · AIO**

The AIO is the one stage everyone in this room can do. Its score decides who is invited to the December School of Excellence. AMT does not publish the cut-off score.

**Slide 6 · Format and skills**

There is no official AIO syllabus. The P1–6 topic list comes from past papers, 2019 to 2026. Entry costs AUD 25.90 per student in 2026 and the entry deadline was a week before the contest.

**Slide 7 · School of Excellence**

This is a school, not a contest: lectures, labs and practice contests. Only students invited here can later make the IOI team.

**Slide 8 · Format and what you will learn**

The aim of the school is to take a student from "can solve AIO Problem 6" to "can attempt invitational problems" in time for the February AIIO, about eight weeks later. C++ becomes the practical choice from here on: the AIIO and the IOI accept only C++.

**Slide 9 · AIIO and FARIO**

AIIO = Australian Invitational Informatics Olympiad. FARIO = French-Australian Regional Informatics Olympiad. Invitation to the AIIO is based on AIO performance; a student's relative AIIO score is a main criterion for the next step.

**Slide 10 · AIIO and FARIO side by side**

Both are sat at school under supervision. Problems come in roughly increasing difficulty. Input and output are standard input and output, with no code templates. Students may not bring in saved code, so they need to be able to write their own brute force for testing.

**Slide 11 · Topics in recent papers**

All of these problems have solutions in our AIIO folder. The early subtasks of most problems need only AIO-level techniques; the full solutions need the graph, DP and data-structure material from the School of Excellence.

**Slide 12 · What the AIIO rewards**

These six habits matter as much as the topics. Ask students which ones they already do in the AIO. Kth Number (2025 P4) adds one more: an interactive task where full marks need at most ⌈N/2⌉+1 queries, and the grader may adapt its answers.

**Slide 13 · Selection School**

The 2026 Informatics Selection School ran from 23 April to 1 May at UNSW, Sydney. Invitations are based on AIIO and FARIO results.

**Slide 14 · Format and skills**

At this level few new topics are taught; the school is about applying known material creatively to IOI-difficulty problems. IOI tasks usually ask students to write a function that a grader calls, instead of reading standard input.

**Slide 15 · IOI**

Every country sends up to four secondary-school students. The IOI is held mid-year, usually between July and September.

**Slide 16 · The contest and the syllabus**

The syllabus marks each topic as included, outside of focus or excluded. The "left out" topics are explicitly excluded: knowing them gives no advantage at the IOI. The full summary is in the selection pathway document.

**Slide 17 · Your toolkit and what comes next**

The left card is what Lessons 1 to 4 covered. The right card is the gap between AIO Problem 6 and the AIIO. Ask: which of the right-hand topics has anyone already seen?

**Slide 18 · Three things to remember**

Ask each student to say which stage they are aiming for this year.

**Slide 19 · Before the next AIO**

2025 P1 Buildings needs only a counting array and a careful loop, so it is a good first AIIO problem for students who finished the P6 lessons.

**Slide 20 · How the syllabus labels topics**

Source: IOI Syllabus 2025, ioinformatics.org/files/ioi-syllabus-2025.pdf. The syllabus is updated each year by the International Scientific Committee; check the latest edition before relying on a detail.

**Slide 21 · Maths, programming and strategies**

Most of the excluded maths (modular inverses, trigonometry, FFT) is common in other contests such as Codeforces, but will not be needed at the IOI.

**Slide 22 · Algorithms and data structures**

The syllabus does not name segment trees or Fenwick trees directly; earlier editions listed them under statically balanced and augmented binary search trees.
